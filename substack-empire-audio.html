Building an Empire on the Back of Substack

A strategic report, prepared as a narration script for audio reading.

A note on how to listen. This is a spoken-word adaptation of a research report. It walks through seven case studies of media empires built during earlier platform shifts, distills the pattern they share, and then applies that pattern to a portfolio of brands being built on Substack today. There are no tables, links, or footnotes to read aloud. Numbers are approximate unless stated otherwise.

The core idea

Every platform shift mints a few empires, and the window to claim one is short. It is fair to say that almost no one is thinking about Substack the way smart operators thought about Instagram in twenty eleven, or YouTube in twenty thirteen, or email newsletters in twenty sixteen. That gap is the opportunity. But the fact that there is no obvious precedent on Substack itself does not mean there is no precedent at all. Across every prior wave of new platforms, there is a remarkably consistent pattern for who wins the land grab, and who gets washed out when the platform's favor inevitably changes.

Here is the single most important finding, stated first, because everything else hangs off it. Borrowed attention is only valuable if you convert it into something you own. That means an email list you can take with you. It means brands and intellectual property that live off the platform. And it means more than one source of revenue. Every durable empire did these things. And every collapse, from the YouTube networks to the TikTok houses to the venture-funded digital media roll-ups, failed for precisely the same reason. They scaled attention on a platform they did not control, and never converted that attention into assets they owned.

Substack is unusually well suited to this strategy, for one structural reason. You own, and you can export, your email list. That makes a Substack audience much closer to the newsletter empires, like Morning Brew and Axios, than to the rented audiences on Instagram or TikTok that died with their algorithms. Substack carries its own risks, and we will come to those. But the foundation is sound.

The pattern behind every platform-shift empire

Strip away the specifics, and the same six-move sequence recurs across Instagram, YouTube, newsletters, and TikTok.

Move one. Arrive early, while the platform is underpriced and competition is thin. Move two. Aggregate attention cheaply, with a content engine that compounds rather than one that has to be rebuilt every time. Move three, and this is the crucial one. Convert that attention into an audience you actually own, through email, logins, and first-party data you can carry off the platform. Move four. Diversify revenue, across subscriptions, sponsorship, commerce, events, and licensing, so that no single channel is a point of failure. Move five. Roll up or incubate adjacent properties and talent, but only by adding real value, never as a middleman who simply takes a cut. And move six. Build a brand bigger than the platform, with owned products that would survive if the platform vanished tomorrow.

Everything that follows is just this sequence, proven out one case at a time, and then applied to your own portfolio.

Case one. FuckJerry, and the Instagram land grab

This is the purest example of arriving early and aggregating attention, and also the purest cautionary tale about how you choose to aggregate it. Around twenty eleven, Elliot Tebele started the account known as FuckJerry, and grew it to roughly fourteen million Instagram followers by twenty eighteen, almost entirely by reposting other people's memes and jokes. That was a near-zero-cost content engine, exploiting an underpriced window before crediting norms and competition caught up.

He then converted the audience three ways. A branded content agency, charging around thirty thousand dollars per sponsored post, for clients like Coca-Cola and Bud Light. Account management services. And, most importantly, owned products. The card game What Do You Meme launched in twenty sixteen, sold out its crowdfunding campaign in hours, and became a top seller. Its parent company, Relatable, now reportedly generates more than one hundred million dollars a year. He also launched a tequila brand. Those products are the lesson worth copying. They are platform-independent assets, and the real durable value was built off Instagram entirely.

Now the landmines. The whole model rested on reposting other people's work without credit or payment. That drew lawsuits, and in twenty nineteen it triggered a viral boycott led by prominent comedians, including John Mulaney and Patton Oswalt. Comedy Central pulled its advertising. The reputation debt, tolerated for years, detonated the moment the brand was a big enough target. The apology, a promise to start asking permission, arrived only after the advertising money was threatened, and it pointedly said nothing about paying creators. On top of that, the company marketed the fraudulent Fyre Festival, and then co-produced the documentary about it. The transferable lesson for Substack is simple. Aggregation works, but do it as curation with credit and revenue sharing, lifting other writers rather than strip-mining them. And the move of building owned products off the audience is one you should copy directly.

Case two. Barstool Sports, and the personality network

This is the closest structural model for the kind of empire we are describing. Take one media brand, and turn it into a constellation of personality-led properties. Dave Portnoy founded Barstool in two thousand three as a printed gambling sheet, took it online around two thousand seven, and rode every shift after that, from blog to podcasts to social to streaming, without losing the core voice. He built a network of distinct shows and personalities, each owning a niche, all monetized by an in-house advertising operation, and bound together by a fiercely loyal community.

The ownership story is instructive. The gambling company Penn bought a thirty-six percent stake in twenty twenty, at a valuation around four hundred fifty million dollars, then bought the rest, for a combined total of roughly five hundred fifty million dollars. Then, in twenty twenty-three, Penn sold the whole thing back to Portnoy for one dollar, taking a write-off of more than eight hundred million dollars, because the edgy, founder-driven brand turned out to be incompatible with gambling-license requirements.

Two lessons matter most. First, talent will outgrow you, so own the intellectual property early. Barstool launched the hit podcast Call Her Daddy but did not lock down the talent or the rights. Its host, Alex Cooper, walked to Spotify for around sixty million dollars, then to SiriusXM for up to one hundred twenty-five million dollars, and built her own network. The platform captured the launch, but the individual captured the empire. Second, founder and brand-safety risk caps your ceiling. Portnoy is both the brand and the liability, and persistent controversy suppressed premium advertiser revenue. The thing to copy is the portfolio of personality brands and the tribal community that drives commerce. The thing to avoid is tying the whole enterprise to one person's reputation, and letting talent build leverage you do not share in.

Case three. The newsletter empires, your closest analog

Email-native media is the most direct precedent for a Substack empire, because the asset being built, and later bought, is an owned and addressable list. That is exactly what Substack gives you.

Consider the exits. Morning Brew, founded in twenty fifteen, grew past four million subscribers, built a portfolio of business verticals plus podcasts, events, and courses, became profitable, and sold a majority stake to Insider in twenty twenty at a valuation around seventy-five million dollars. Axios, founded in twenty sixteen, built a national brand plus a network of local city newsletters, and sold to Cox Enterprises in twenty twenty-two at a valuation around five hundred twenty-five million dollars. Industry Dive, a business-to-business roll-up of more than seventy newsletters, sold to Informa for three hundred eighty-nine million dollars, rising toward five hundred twenty-five million with earn-outs. The Hustle sold to HubSpot in twenty twenty-one for a reported twenty-seven million dollars. And theSkimm sold to Ziff Davis in twenty twenty-five, on undisclosed terms, after years of shopping itself as its audience plateaued.

The repeatable playbook is clear. Own the list, because that is literally the asset acquirers buy, an audience plus first-party data with no algorithm in between. Build a portfolio of titles, not a single newsletter, so you can cross-promote off a flagship and sell niche audiences at higher rates. Stack multiple revenue lines, across advertising, premium subscriptions, events, courses, and podcasts. Reach profitability, because that is what turns a newsletter into an acquisition. And sell to a strategic buyer whose core business is amplified by owning your list. The cautionary notes are equally clear. Sell from strength, not after the audience has stalled, as theSkimm learned. A single list monetized only by advertising is a fine cash business, but it caps your options and your eventual value. And never confuse reach with revenue. Buyers diligence engaged, deliverable, paying subscribers, not headline numbers.

Case four. MrBeast versus the YouTube networks

This is the clearest do-this and never-do-that pair in the entire report. On the do-this side is Jimmy Donaldson, known as MrBeast, who crossed five hundred million YouTube subscribers in twenty twenty-six. His key move was not taking brand-deal fees. It was building fully owned, vertically integrated consumer brands. His chocolate company, Feastables, did around two hundred fifty million dollars in revenue and roughly twenty million dollars in profit in twenty twenty-four. By contrast, his media arm actually loses money, around eighty million dollars in the same year. The content is the flywheel. The owned product is the margin. The whole enterprise was valued around five point two billion dollars. And note the cautionary counter-example embedded inside his own story. His burger venture failed, because he licensed his name into someone else's operation, did not own it, and ended up in litigation. The lesson he learned, and you should too, is to own the product and the intellectual property outright.

On the never-do-that side are the YouTube multi-channel networks. Disney bought Maker Studios in twenty fourteen for up to roughly nine hundred fifty million dollars including earn-outs, then wrote it down and gutted it within a few years. Its peers, Machinima, Fullscreen, and Defy Media, all collapsed. They failed for one reason. They aggregated thousands of creators as a pure middleman, took a cut of the revenue, added little real value, had no lock-in because creators could simply leave, and owned no intellectual property of their own. When the platform tightened its advertising economics, their thin margins vanished overnight. This is the single biggest trap for the idea of an empire built on other creators. Rolling up Substack writers as a middleman who takes a cut and adds nothing is the Maker Studios model, and it fails every time.

Case five. TikTok houses versus brand builders

The most recent wave teaches the same lesson. The collab houses, like Hype House, rose fast and collapsed fast. They had no real business model, no owned product, and the structural killer was that individual stars outgrew the collective and left. Compare that to the creators who built ownable brands. The D'Amelio family launched a holding company with real equity in footwear, skincare, and fragrance, explicitly because, in their words, fame comes and goes. Emma Chamberlain built Chamberlain Coffee, an owned consumer brand doing more than twenty million dollars a year, rather than just collecting brand-deal fees. The one-line synthesis across both YouTube and TikTok is this. Own the intellectual property and the product, or be the disposable middleman. Remember that MrBeast's media arm loses money. What makes his company worth billions is the chocolate he owns outright.

Case six. The digital media roll-up bust

An entire generation tried exactly the empire-of-many-properties model, and most of it was written down to nearly nothing. Understanding why is how you avoid repeating it. Gawker pioneered the network-of-verticals model, and a single lawsuit, with a one hundred forty million dollar judgment, killed the whole company. BuzzFeed built its business on Facebook's algorithm and cratered when Facebook changed it, falling from a peak valuation around one point seven billion dollars to a market value around thirty million. Vice Media rode a valuation of five point seven billion dollars straight into bankruptcy.

They nearly all failed for the same handful of reasons. They depended on Facebook and Google for traffic, so when the algorithm changed, the audience and the business vanished. They relied on commodity advertising revenue at low and falling rates. They had no direct relationship with their audience, no email, no subscriptions, no first-party data. And crucially, diversifying their content into many verticals did not diversify their revenue, because every brand still fed off the same advertising pipe. The survivors did the opposite. The Atlantic and the New York Times owned their audiences and charged them directly through subscriptions. The warning, in one sentence, is this. Many brands on someone else's pipe, monetized by commodity advertising, is the exact failure pattern of two decades of digital media. A Substack empire must be the opposite. Owned lists, direct subscription revenue, and diversified income.

The winning playbook, distilled

Here is the synthesis, as a checklist you can hold every decision against. The things to do. Arrive early and aggregate while attention is underpriced, which is exactly where Substack is now. Own a portable audience, the email list you can export, as your non-negotiable core asset. Build a portfolio of niche brands that cross-promote off a flagship. Add real value to any talent you bring in, never just take a cut. Diversify revenue early, across subscriptions, sponsorship, commerce, events, and licensing. Build owned products off the platform that would survive without it. Reach profitability, because it is leverage and optionality. And always keep the ability to leave on your own terms.

And the things to avoid. Do not roll up creators as a value-less middleman. Do not depend on one platform's algorithm for distribution, or one channel for revenue. Do not strip-mine other people's work, because reputation debt detonates at scale. Do not let talent build leverage you do not share in. Do not tie the empire to one founder's controversy. Do not confuse reach with revenue. Do not sell or raise money from a position of weakness after your audience has plateaued. And do not over-raise against hype, inflating a valuation you cannot grow into.

Substack itself, and its risks

A few facts about the platform you are building on. Substack takes ten percent of subscription revenue, and the payment processor takes roughly another three percent, so you keep around eighty-five percent. The company was valued at one point one billion dollars in a funding round in mid twenty twenty-five, reached positive cash flow for the first time in early twenty twenty-five, and has more than five million paid subscriptions across the platform, with over fifty writers earning more than a million dollars a year from subscriptions alone. Its discovery features, the Notes feed, the recommendations engine, and the bestseller leaderboards, are designed to drive cross-subscriptions, which is rocket fuel for a multi-property strategy.

The decisive structural advantage, again, is that Substack lets you export your full subscriber list. That is why this strategy is viable here, and was not for the TikTok houses. But keep your eyes open to the risks. The platform was unprofitable until very recently and is still growing into its valuation. It has had content-moderation controversies that caused some writers to leave, which means reputational contagion is a real risk for anyone whose brand sits on it. And while your email list is portable, the discovery engine is not. If you leave, you keep the list but lose the recommendations and the reach. So keep credible alternatives, like Ghost and beehiiv, as live options, and make leaving a real capability rather than an empty threat.

Your actual portfolio

Now to your specific situation, which is stronger than most. These are not other people's accounts. They are your own brands, and together they already form the shape of an empire that most operators never manage to assemble. You have a flagship voice, an owned product, a talent property, community layers, and a personal holding brand. The strategic question is not what to build. It is focus, sequencing, and how the pieces reinforce each other.

Start with Gallop, your idea for a Letterboxd for galleries. This is your crown jewel. It is the one asset you own outright, and the one that would survive if Substack disappeared. It is the equivalent of MrBeast's chocolate brand. Everything else should funnel audience and data into it, and it deserves priority above the newsletters.

Francis Sable, your pseudonymous outlet for candid thoughts on the art world, is your flagship voice and attention engine. The pseudonym is a smart move. It lets you be genuinely candid without the personal reputation risk that capped a brand like Barstool. The trade-off is that it is firewalled from your other brands. It cannot openly cross-promote them without linking your identities, and it cannot carry a product tied to a face. So treat it as a powerful standalone engine, and plan now for the risk of being unmasked.

The artist account you run for Maja is a talent property, and it carries the Call Her Daddy lesson directly. Agree on ownership, revenue splits, and what happens if she leaves, in writing, now, while the relationship is friendly. You are the launchpad, so make sure you share in the upside you are creating. Lobster Club is your community and events layer. Events and real-world community are high-margin, they are the deepest competitive moat, and they are very much on-theme for an art-world audience. Alaya, your spiritual recovery community, is an adjacent bet with a different audience. That is fine, but it will not cross-promote cleanly with the art brands, so treat it as its own venture or build the bridge deliberately. And T's New Thing is your personal brand for products and invention, the public-facing umbrella that ties your non-pseudonymous pieces together.

Five things to internalize about your setup. First, you already have the shape of an empire, so the real risk is focus. Six properties run by one person is the spreading-thin failure mode that killed the collab houses. Sequence them. Do not run all six at full tilt. Second, your through-line is the art world. Francis Sable, Maja, Gallop, and most likely Lobster Club share one audience, and that coherence is exactly the acquirable asset the newsletter empires had. Alaya and T's New Thing sit outside it, so decide consciously whether they belong in the empire or are separate bets. Third, Gallop is your owned product, so prioritize it, and use the art-world newsletters as the cheap top of the funnel that feeds it. Fourth, you are your own network, so bundle. Your properties can cross-recommend for free, and you can offer a paid bundle, with the one caveat that the pseudonymous brand stays separate. And fifth, lock down the terms with Maja now, because the talent relationship is the single most predictable future conflict in your portfolio.

Your sequenced action plan

Finally, the build order. The sequencing matters. Own the asset first, diversify revenue second, expand the network third, and build off-platform intellectual property fourth.

Step one. Win one flagship and define the audience. Before any empire, you need one publication with a coherent, valuable, namable audience, the thing every newsletter winner had. Get it to genuine product-market fit and a real paid-conversion rate before you expand. Do not spread thin.

Step two. Lock down ownership of the asset from day one. Export your subscriber list every month and keep it off the platform. Treat the email list, not your follower count, as the company. Stand up a lightweight backup on your own domain, so that leaving is always a real option. This is the single move that separates you from every TikTok house that died.

Step three. Diversify revenue before you scale up headcount. Stack at least three lines early. Paid subscriptions as the base. Native, audience-fit sponsorship, which earns more than commodity advertising because your niche is well defined. And one of events, commerce, or a paid community. Reach profitability before you raise any outside money.

Step four. Build the network as a value-adding collective, not a tax. This is where you either become Morning Brew or become a failed YouTube network. As you bring other voices under the umbrella, starting with Maja, win them by giving them something they cannot get alone. Cross-promotion off your flagship. Shared sponsorship sales at rates they could not command on their own. Production support. A bundle. Share revenue transparently. The moment you are simply taking a cut and adding nothing, you have built a doomed middleman business.

Step five. Build owned intellectual property that survives the platform. In every case study, the durable value lived off the platform. For you, that is Gallop above all, and potentially a real-world event franchise, which is highly on-brand for an art audience. This is the thing that makes the whole enterprise worth more than its newsletter revenue, and that cannot be taken away by an algorithm change.

Step six. Protect against the known failure modes, deliberately. Define intellectual property and revenue terms with any talent up front. Credit and pay everyone whose work you feature, and do not tie the whole brand to one person's controversy. Keep your exit ramp built and tested, given Substack's history. And grow into any valuation rather than over-raising against hype.

Here is the empire in one sentence. Use Substack to cheaply aggregate a defined, valuable audience that you can own and export. Lift a network of adjacent voices by genuinely adding value. Diversify your revenue early. And convert all of it into off-platform intellectual property that would survive if Substack disappeared tomorrow. Do that, and you are Morning Brew. Skip the owning part, or the adding-real-value part, and you are just a TikTok house with a newsletter.

End of report.
