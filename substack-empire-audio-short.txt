Building an Empire on the Back of Substack

A short strategic briefing, written for audio.

The core idea. Every platform shift mints a few empires, and the window is short. Almost no one is thinking about Substack the way operators thought about Instagram in twenty eleven, or newsletters in twenty sixteen. That gap is the opportunity. And there is a precedent, even if not on Substack itself. Across every prior wave, the same rule decides who wins. Borrowed attention is only valuable if you convert it into something you own. An email list you can take with you. Brands and products that live off the platform. And more than one source of revenue. Every durable empire did this. Every collapse failed for the same reason. They scaled attention on a platform they did not control, and never turned it into assets they owned.

Substack is well suited to this for one reason. You own, and can export, your email list. That makes it closer to the newsletter empires than to Instagram or TikTok, where audiences died with the algorithm.

What the case studies teach. Five quick lessons.

One. Build owned products off the audience. FuckJerry grew to fourteen million Instagram followers reposting memes, then built a card game whose parent company now makes more than one hundred million dollars a year. The durable value was off the platform. But the model rested on stealing other people's work, and a comedian-led boycott eventually cost it major advertisers. So aggregate by lifting others with credit and revenue sharing, not by strip-mining them.

Two. A network of personality brands works, but own the talent and the rights. Barstool turned one blog into a constellation of shows, and sold for around five hundred fifty million dollars. But it launched the hit podcast Call Her Daddy without locking down the rights, and the host left for deals worth sixty, then one hundred twenty-five million dollars, taking the value with her. Own the intellectual property early, or accept that you are only a launchpad.

Three. The newsletter empires are your closest analog, and they sold for real money. Morning Brew, around seventy-five million dollars. Axios, around five hundred twenty-five million. Industry Dive, similar. The playbook. Own the list. Build a portfolio of titles, not one. Stack revenue across subscriptions, sponsorship, events, and courses. Reach profitability. And sell to a buyer whose business your audience makes stronger.

Four. Own the product, or be the disposable middleman. MrBeast's media arm loses money. What makes his company worth billions is the chocolate brand he owns outright. Meanwhile the YouTube networks that rolled up thousands of creators as a pure middleman, taking a cut and adding nothing, all collapsed. Rolling up Substack writers that way is the same doomed model. The TikTok houses died the same way, while creators who built their own brands, like Chamberlain Coffee, endured.

Five. Do not depend on someone else's pipe. An entire generation of digital media, from Gawker to BuzzFeed to Vice, was written down to almost nothing, because they relied on Facebook and Google for traffic and on commodity advertising for revenue, with no direct relationship to their audience. Owned lists and direct subscription revenue are the whole difference.

Your portfolio. This is the strong part. These are your own brands, and together they already form the shape of an empire. The question is not what to build. It is focus and sequencing.

Gallop, your Letterboxd for galleries, is the crown jewel. It is the one thing you own outright that survives if Substack disappears. Prioritize it, and use the newsletters to feed it. Francis Sable, your pseudonymous art-world voice, is your attention engine. The pseudonym protects you from personal reputation risk, but it is walled off from your other brands and cannot cross-promote them openly. The artist account you run for Maja is a talent property. Agree on ownership and revenue splits in writing now, while it is friendly. Lobster Club is your community and events layer, high margin and on-theme for the art world. Alaya, your spiritual community, is a separate bet with a different audience. And T's New Thing is the personal umbrella that ties your public brands together.

The real risk is focus. Six properties run by one person is the spreading-thin failure mode that killed the collab houses. Your through-line is the art world. Francis Sable, Maja, Gallop, and Lobster Club share an audience, and that coherence is the acquirable asset. Sequence them. Do not run all six at full tilt.

The plan, in order. First, win one flagship with a clear, valuable audience before expanding. Second, own the asset. Export your list every month and keep a backup off the platform, so leaving is always a real option. Third, diversify revenue early, and reach profitability before raising any money. Fourth, grow the network by adding real value to other voices, never just taking a cut. Fifth, build owned intellectual property that survives the platform, starting with Gallop and a real-world event series. And sixth, protect against the known traps. Lock down talent terms, credit and pay your contributors, and keep your exit ramp ready.

The empire in one sentence. Use Substack to cheaply build a defined audience you can own and export, lift a network of voices by genuinely adding value, diversify revenue early, and convert all of it into off-platform property that would survive if Substack vanished tomorrow. Do that, and you are Morning Brew. Skip the owning, and you are just a TikTok house with a newsletter.

End of briefing.
