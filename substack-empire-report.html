Building an Empire on the Back of Substack

What every platform-shift winner did right, what killed the ones who flamed out, and a concrete playbook for owning the Substack wave before anyone else figures out it's a wave.

Prepared June 2026· 7 case studies· ~60 sourced data points
The Thesis

Every platform shift mints a few empires. The window is short and it's open now.

You're right that almost no one is thinking about Substack the way operators thought about Instagram in 2012, YouTube in 2013, or email newsletters in 2016. That's the opportunity. But "no precedent on Substack" doesn't mean no precedent — there's a very consistent pattern across every prior wave for who wins the land grab and who gets washed out when the platform's favor changes.

This report reverse-engineers that pattern from seven empires (and a graveyard of cautionary tales), then translates it into a Substack-specific plan. The single most important finding, stated up front so everything else hangs off it:

The one rule that separates empires from flameouts

Borrowed attention is only valuable if you convert it into something you own — an email list you can port, IP/brands that live off-platform, and multiple revenue lines. Every durable empire did this. Every collapse — the MCNs, the TikTok houses, the VC-funded digital-media roll-ups — failed precisely because they scaled attention on a platform they didn't control and never converted it into owned, margin-bearing assets.

Substack is unusually good for this strategy for one structural reason: you own and can export your email list. That makes it closer to the newsletter empires (Morning Brew, Axios) than to Instagram or TikTok — but it carries its own platform risks, covered in §8.

01 · The Pattern

How platform-shift empires actually get built

Strip away the specifics and the same six-move sequence recurs across Instagram, YouTube, newsletters, and TikTok:

MoveWhat the winners didWhat the losers did
1. Arrive earlyBuilt audience while the platform was underpriced and crediting/competition norms were weak (FuckJerry 2011, Barstool's 2007 web pivot, Morning Brew 2015).Arrived after the land was claimed and paid retail for attention.
2. Aggregate attention cheaplyA scalable, low-cost content engine — repackaging, a daily format, a repeatable show.Expensive bespoke content with no compounding loop.
3. Convert to owned audienceEmail lists, logged-in subscribers, first-party data they could port off-platform.Rented audiences that lived inside Facebook/YouTube/TikTok's graph.
4. Diversify revenueAds + subscriptions + commerce/CPG + events + licensing — no single point of failure.One revenue line (usually programmatic ads or platform payouts).
5. Roll up or incubate adjacencyA network of niche brands/talent under one umbrella, cross-promoting cheaply.Rolled up creators as a pure middleman, adding no value (MCNs).
6. Build a brand bigger than the platformOwned IP and products that would survive if the platform vanished (Feastables, What Do You Meme?, Chamberlain Coffee).A brand that was the platform presence and died with the algorithm.

The rest of this report is just this table, proven out case by case — and then applied to Substack.

02 · Case Study

FuckJerry & Jerry Media — the Instagram land grab (and its landmines)

The purest example of arriving early and aggregating attention — and the purest cautionary tale about how you aggregate it.

2011
Elliot Tebele starts @fuckjerry
~14M
IG followers by 2018–19
~$30K
per sponsored post (Adweek)
$100M+
annual revenue of the products arm (Relatable)

What they built

Tebele grew one of Instagram's largest accounts by reposting/aggregating other people's memes and jokes — a near-zero-cost content engine exploiting an underpriced 2011–2015 attention window. He then converted the audience three ways: a premium branded-content agency (Jerry Media, ~$30K/post, clients incl. Coca-Cola, Subway, Bud Light), account-takeover services, and — crucially — owned products.

The owned products are the part worth studying. What Do You Meme? (2016) launched on Kickstarter, hit its goal in ~4 hours, raised ~$230K, and became a top-selling Amazon party game. Its parent company, Relatable, now reportedly does $100M+ in annual revenue across 45M+ units and 100+ products. They also launched JAJA Tequila (2018). These are platform-independent, margin-bearing assets — the durable value was built off Instagram.

✓ What to copy

  • Early-mover aggregation of an underpriced attention surface.
  • Productize the audience into owned IP (a card game, a spirit brand) — not just brand-deal fees.
  • Use the audience as a launch channel (sold out Kickstarter in hours).
  • Stack revenue: agency + services + products.

✕ The landmines

  • IP/plagiarism as the engine. Reposting uncredited work drew lawsuits and the viral #FuckFuckJerry boycott (2019) led by John Mulaney, Patton Oswalt et al. Comedy Central pulled its ads.
  • Reputation debt compounds — tolerated for years, then detonated the moment they were a big enough target.
  • Reactive apologies ring hollow — the "we'll get permission" pledge came only after ad money was threatened, and pointedly didn't mention paying creators.
  • Toxic association — marketing Fyre Festival, then co-producing the Netflix doc about it.

Substack lesson: aggregation works, but on Substack do it as curation with credit and revenue-share — a network that lifts writers, not one that strip-mines them. The "build owned products off the audience" move is directly portable. Sources: Adweek, Wikipedia, AOL/Finance, KnowYourMeme.

03 · Case Study

Barstool Sports — the personality-network empire

The closest structural model for what you're describing: turn one media brand into a constellation of personality-led properties. Also a masterclass in talent leverage and founder risk.

2003
Founded as a print gambling sheet
~$450M

~$551M → $1
PENN bought in, then sold back to Portnoy
100M+
monthly uniques (2024–25)

The model

Portnoy rode every platform shift — print → blog (~2007) → podcasts → social → streaming — without losing the core voice, and built a network of niche personality brands under one umbrella: Pardon My Take, KFC Radio, Call Her Daddy, Rough N' Rowdy PPV boxing, the One Bite pizza app, Barstool Gold subscriptions, merch (~$60–80M/yr), and gambling. Each show owns a distinct niche; the in-house ad network monetizes the whole portfolio; the "Stoolies" community drives commerce that ad-only media can't.

The two lessons that matter most for you

✕ Talent will outgrow you — own the IP early

Barstool launched Call Her Daddy but didn't lock the talent or own durable IP. Alex Cooper walked to Spotify (~$60M), then SiriusXM (up to ~$125M), and built her own Unwell Network. The platform captured the launch; the individual captured the empire.

✕ Founder/brand-safety risk caps your ceiling

Portnoy is the brand and the liability. Persistent controversy suppressed blue-chip advertiser revenue, and the edgy brand proved regulatorily incompatible with gambling — forcing PENN to write off $800M+ and hand Barstool back for $1.

✓ What to copy

A portfolio of personality brands de-risks any single one. A tribal community (not just an audience) unlocks commerce, PPV, subscriptions, and apps. Diversify revenue aggressively so no single channel is a point of failure. But if you incubate stars: own durable IP or accept you're a launchpad, not the destination.

Sources: CNBC, Variety, Variety (Cooper/SiriusXM).

04 · Case Study

The newsletter empires — your closest analog

Email-native media is the most direct precedent for a Substack empire, because the asset being built (and later bought) is an owned, addressable list — exactly what Substack gives you.

CompanyBuiltExit
Morning Brew (2015)2.5M→4M+ subs; B2B "Brew" verticals (Marketing, Tech, Retail, HR, CFO…); podcasts, events, courses. Profitable since 2018.Majority to Insider/Axel Springer (2020) at ~$75M; Axel Springer took the rest in 2025. On track for ~$70M+ revenue in 2025.

theSkimm (2012)~5–7M subs across 6 newsletters; Skimm Money/Well; ambassador program; raised ~$29M.Sold to Ziff Davis / Everyday Health (2025), terms undisclosed — read as a soft outcome after years of shopping it.

✓ The repeatable playbook

  • Own the list — that's literally the asset acquirers buy (audience + first-party data, no algorithm gatekeeper).
  • A portfolio of titles, not one newsletter — cross-promote off the flagship, sell niche audiences at higher CPMs, de-risk any single title.
  • Stack revenue: ads + premium subs + events + courses + podcasts + SaaS spinouts.
  • Be profitable — it's what turns a newsletter into an acquisition.
  • Sell to a strategic whose core business is amplified by your list.

✕ What to avoid

  • Sell from strength, not after the plateau — theSkimm shopped itself for years as subs slid from ~7M to ~5M.
  • One list + ads only (the 1440 model) is a great cash business but caps optionality and exit multiple — thin first-party data, nothing to upsell.
  • Reach ≠ revenue. Acquirers diligence engaged, deliverable, monetizable subscribers, not headline counts.

The strategic-buyer logic is the punchline: HubSpot bought a top-of-funnel audience for its software; Informa bought a B2B audience to feed events; Ziff Davis bought a female health/wellness audience. A Substack network with a defined, valuable audience is an acquisition target for exactly these reasons. Sources: Axios, CNBC, Axios, Nieman Lab.

05 · Case Study

MrBeast vs. the MCN graveyard — own the product, or be the middleman

The clearest "do this / never do that" pair in the whole report.

Do this

MrBeast / Beast Industries

Optimized obsessively for retention, reinvested everything for years, hit 500M+ YouTube subscribers (2026). The key move: he didn't just take brand-deal fees — he built fully-owned, vertically-integrated CPG brands.

  • Feastables (chocolate, 2022): ~$250M revenue and ~$20M profit in 2024; projected ~$520M in 2025. This is the engine.
  • The media arm loses money (~$250M revenue, ~$80M net loss in 2024). Content is the flywheel; the owned product is the margin.
  • 2024 raise: ~$5.2B valuation (Alpha Wave).

Counter-example inside the example: MrBeast Burger — he licensed his name into someone else's operation (Virtual Dining Concepts), didn't own it, called the food "inedible," and ended up in litigation. The lesson he internalized and you should too: own the IP and the product.

Never do this

Maker Studios & the MCNs

Disney bought Maker Studios (2014) for ~$500M (up to ~$950M w/ earnouts); it aggregated tens of thousands of YouTube channels. By 2017 Disney had written off ~$100M and gutted it. Machinima, Fullscreen, Defy Media (collapsed 2018, owing creators) — the whole category died.

Why MCNs failed — the roll-up trap

  • Took a cut of creator revenue while adding little value.
  • No leverage / no lock-in — creators owned their audiences and could leave.
  • No owned IP — scale without ownership.
  • Platform controlled the economics — the 2017 "Adpocalypse" crushed thin margins overnight.

This is the single biggest trap for your idea. "Rolling up Substack writers" as a middleman who takes a cut and adds nothing is the Maker Studios model. It fails every time.

Sources: Fortune, Variety, TechCrunch, Digiday.

06 · Case Study

TikTok houses vs. brand-builders — collectives collapse, owned brands endure

The most recent wave, and the one whose mistakes are freshest.

✕ The collab houses (fast rise, fast collapse)

Hype House (Dec 2019 — Charli & Dixie D'Amelio, Addison Rae) got a Netflix show in Jan 2022 after the stars had already left, and dissolved. Sway House disbanded Feb 2021 amid arrests and chaos. Clubhouse: dead by late 2021.

Why: no real business model, no owned product, internal conflict, and — the structural killer — individual stars outgrew the collective and left. Same defect as the MCNs: no lock-in, no owned IP.

✓ The brand-builders (durable)

  • The D'Amelios: a Dunkin' drink that spiked sales 45%, a Hulu show, and D'Amelio Brands (equity, not fees) — footwear, skincare, fragrance. Stated thesis: "Fame comes and goes" → build ownable equity.
  • Emma Chamberlain: Chamberlain Coffee (~$22M rev in 2024) — an owned CPG brand, not a brand deal.
  • Alix Earle: even her network deal (Alex Cooper's Unwell) ended with Unwell renouncing all rights so Earle could own the show — the creator kept the durable asset.
The one-line synthesis across YouTube and TikTok: Own the IP and the product, or be the disposable middleman. MrBeast's media arm loses ~$80M/year — what makes Beast Industries a $5.2B company is the chocolate he owns outright.

Sources: Dazed, Fox Business, Variety.

07 · Case Study

The digital-media roll-up bust — the "network of verticals" cautionary tale

An entire generation tried exactly the "empire of many properties" model you're describing — and most of it was written down to zero. Understanding why is how you avoid repeating it.

$5.7B → bankrupt
Vice Media (2017 peak → 2023 Ch.11, sold for ~$225–350M)
$1.7B → ~$30M
BuzzFeed (peak private valuation → 2024 market cap)
$140M
judgment that bankrupted Gawker Media (2016)

Gawker pioneered the network-of-verticals model (Gizmodo, Deadspin, Jezebel, Kotaku, Lifehacker) — and a single Thiel-funded lawsuit killed the whole company. BuzzFeed built on Facebook's algorithm and cratered when Facebook changed it. Vice rode a $5.7B valuation into bankruptcy. Group Nine (NowThis, The Dodo, Thrillist, Seeker) rolled up verticals and got absorbed into Vox. The "pivot to video" (2017) wiped out Mashable, Mic, Refinery29.

Why nearly all of them failed

  • Platform dependency for traffic. Audiences belonged to Facebook/Google. When the algorithm changed, the traffic — and the business — vanished. No owned distribution.
  • Commodity programmatic ad revenue. Low, falling CPMs in a market the duopoly priced.
  • No direct audience relationship — no email, no logins, no subscriptions, no first-party data, no recurring revenue. Vice's bankruptcy filing literally blamed having "no consumer revenue stream."
  • Diversifying content verticals didn't diversify revenue — every brand fed off the same ad/traffic pipe.
  • Concentrated legal/founder risk across one balance sheet (Gawker).

✓ What the survivors did differently

The Atlantic (1M+ subscribers, profitable 2024) and the NYT (games, cooking, audio bundles) owned their audiences and charged them directly. Owned relationships + diversified revenue beyond programmatic ads is the entire difference between the survivors and the write-downs.

The warning for you, in one sentence: "many brands on someone else's pipe, monetized by commodity ads" is the exact failure pattern of 2002–2023 digital media. A Substack empire must be the opposite — owned lists, direct subscription revenue, diversified income. Sources: PBS, THR, Fortune.

08 · Synthesis

The winning playbook, distilled

Across all seven cases, the empires that lasted did the same things. Here's the synthesis as a checklist you can hold every decision against.

✓ DO

  • Arrive early and aggregate while attention is underpriced. (You're here on Substack.)
  • Own a portable audience — the email list you can export — as the non-negotiable core asset.
  • Build a portfolio of niche brands/voices that cross-promote off a flagship.
  • Add real value to any talent you bring in — distribution, production, monetization, audience — never just take a cut.
  • Diversify revenue early: subscriptions + ads/sponsorship + commerce/CPG + events + courses + licensing.
  • Build owned IP/products off-platform (a brand, a product, an event franchise) that survives the platform.
  • Reach profitability — it's leverage, optionality, and the thing that makes you acquirable.
  • Keep off-platform optionality — be able to leave on your terms.

✕ DON'T

  • Don't roll up creators as a value-less middleman (the MCN/collab-house death).
  • Don't depend on one platform's algorithm for distribution or one channel for revenue.
  • Don't strip-mine others' IP — credit and pay; reputation debt detonates at scale (FuckJerry).
  • Don't let talent build leverage you don't share in — own IP or accept you're a launchpad (Call Her Daddy).
  • Don't tie the empire to one founder's controversy — it caps your advertiser ceiling (Barstool).
  • Don't confuse reach with revenue, or vanity subs with engaged ones.
  • Don't sell/raise from weakness after the audience plateaus (theSkimm).
  • Don't over-raise against hype and inflate a valuation you can't grow into (Vice/BuzzFeed).

Substack only reached positive cash flow in Q1 2025, on ~$45M annualized revenue. The top 10 accounts earn ~$40M/year combined. The platform's Notes feed (2023), recommendations engine, and bestseller leaderboards are the discovery loop you can exploit — they're explicitly designed to drive cross-subscriptions, which is rocket fuel for a multi-property strategy.

The decisive structural advantage

Unlike Instagram, YouTube, or TikTok, Substack lets you export your full subscriber list (emails, signup dates, engagement, payment data). That makes a Substack audience closer to the owned newsletter empires than to the rented social-platform audiences that died with their algorithms. This is why the strategy is viable here and wasn't for the TikTok houses.

The risks — eyes open

  • Platform was unprofitable until 2025 and failed to raise a round in 2022. It's better now, but it's a VC-backed company that needs to keep growing into a $1.1B valuation.
  • Content-moderation blowups. The 2023–24 "Substackers Against Nazis" controversy (~250 authors) saw Substack defend a hands-off free-speech stance and only narrowly remove five publications. Casey Newton's Platformer left. Reputational contagion is a real risk for anyone whose brand sits on the platform.
  • Non-portable discovery. Your email list is yours — but the recommendations graph, Notes reach, and leaderboard placement are not. If you leave, you keep the list and lose the discovery engine. Networks that outgrew Substack (the Everything Bundle → Every) rebuilt their own tech.
  • Credible competitors for your exit ramp: Ghost (open-source, 0% rev share, full ownership), beehiiv (built by ex–Morning Brew people, 0% rev share), Patreon. Keep these as live options.

11 · The Plan

A sequenced action plan for your Substack empire

Synthesizing every lesson above into a build order. The sequencing matters: own the asset first, diversify second, expand the network third, build off-platform IP fourth.

1

Win one flagship and define the audience

Before any "empire," you need one publication with a coherent, valuable, namable audience (the thing Morning Brew, Axios, and The Hustle all had). "Young NYC culture/fashion/lifestyle" is a strong, advertiser-legible, acquirer-legible niche. Get the flagship to clear product-market fit and a real paid-conversion rate before expanding — don't spread thin.

2

Lock down ownership of the asset from day one

Export your subscriber list monthly and keep it off-platform. Treat the email list — not your Substack follower count — as the company. Stand up a lightweight off-platform backup (your own domain, an ESP you control, or beehiiv/Ghost as a hedge) so leaving is always a real option, not a threat you can't execute. This is the single move that separates you from every TikTok house that died.

3

Diversify revenue beyond the 90/10 sub before you scale headcount

Stack at minimum three lines early: paid subscriptions (the base), sponsorships (native, audience-fit — higher CPM than programmatic because the niche is defined), and one of events / commerce / a paid community. Profitability is leverage; reach it before raising anything.

4

Build the network as a value-add collective, not a tax

This is where you either become Morning Brew or become Maker Studios. Bring in adjacent writers (the Aliyahs, Mayas, Galas of the scene) by giving them something they can't get alone — cross-promotion off your flagship, shared sponsorship sales at rates they can't command solo, production/editing, a bundle. Use Substack's recommendations engine and Notes as the cheap cross-promotion loop. Share revenue transparently. The moment you're just taking a cut and adding nothing, you've built an MCN and it will collapse.

Structure options, lightest to heaviest: (a) a recommendation alliance (no economics, pure cross-promo); (b) a shared sponsorship/ad network across independent titles; (c) a true bundle (one price, many titles); (d) an equity collective where writers own a piece of the umbrella. Start light, earn the right to go heavier.

5

Build owned IP that survives the platform

The durable value in every case study lived off-platform: What Do You Meme?, Feastables, Chamberlain Coffee, D'Amelio Brands, Axios HQ. Turn your audience into something you own outright — an event/IRL franchise (very on-brand for an NYC culture scene), a product/CPG line, a paid community, a media/IP property, or a SaaS-y tool. This is your Feastables: the thing that makes the whole enterprise worth more than its newsletter revenue, and that can't be taken away by an algorithm change.

6

Protect against the known failure modes — deliberately

  • Talent leverage: if you incubate stars, agree on IP ownership and revenue splits up front (learn from Call Her Daddy).
  • Reputation: credit and pay everyone whose work you feature (learn from FuckJerry); don't tie the whole brand to one person's controversy (learn from Barstool).
  • Platform contagion: have your exit ramp built and tested, given Substack's moderation history.
  • Don't over-raise: grow into any valuation; sell or raise from strength, never after a plateau.

The empire in one sentence

Use Substack to cheaply aggregate a defined, valuable audience you can own and export; lift a network of adjacent voices by genuinely adding value; diversify revenue early; and convert all of it into off-platform IP that would survive if Substack disappeared tomorrow.

Do that, and you're Morning Brew. Skip the "own and export" or the "add real value" parts, and you're a TikTok house with a newsletter.
